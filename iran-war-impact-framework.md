**On the Ground in Iran:** The 88-day internet blackout began lifting May 26 — but the restoration is partial and tightly controlled. Access remains below 10% of pre-shutdown levels, social media is blocked, WhatsApp is restricted, and filtering is more extensive than before the shutdown began. The regime is opening a state-curated propaganda channel while denying Iranians the organizing and verification tools that diaspora media depends on. BBC Persian, Radio Farda, and Iran International remain likely filtered. Economically, dairy prices are confirmed to rise 20% from June 1 as raw milk costs surged 29%; pensioner protests were reported in Shush (May 24), baker protests in Kermanshah (May 23), and student demonstrations in Khorramabad (May 23), all dispersed with tear gas and batons per NCRI. Gross fixed capital formation collapsed to −4.3% in the last reported period, confirming structural economic disintegration. ⚠️ Even with partial internet restoration, independent ground-truth reporting from inside Iran cannot be verified against what state media is now distributing through newly restored channels.

**Gulf States Adaptation:** Qatar's Doha remains the conflict's most operationally loaded address — simultaneously hosting Iran's MOU delegation and absorbing Trump's Abraham Accords demand, which the Qatari Emir must manage without disrupting the mediation framework. Saudi Arabia and UAE overland pipeline diversions (Yanbu, Fujairah, Duqm, Salalah) continue generating positive structural returns independent of Hormuz outcome. Iraq's $17B Development Road — connecting Basra's Faw port to the Turkish border via 1,200 km of rail and highway — is the most significant new Gulf overland infrastructure initiative confirmed in planning, and would structurally reduce Gulf hydrocarbon dependence on Hormuz over a 3–5 year horizon. Qatar LNG has no overland alternative and remains structurally the most exposed Gulf state to prolonged closure.

**Information Warfare:** Iran's partial internet restoration is simultaneously a genuine liberalization signal and a regime information control upgrade: by providing internet access before a deal is announced, the government can now deliver its own narrative of deal terms to Iranian citizens through state-curated channels while blocking diaspora counter-narratives via social media bans and WhatsApp restrictions. Iran's "gross violation" framing of US strikes circulates through PressTV and Fars News and will shape how any deal concessions are presented domestically as "extracted under duress." For Global South audiences, the narrative remains "Iran held the line for 89 days and forced the US to negotiate." Trump's Abraham Accords demand has been absorbed into Iranian state media as evidence of US overreach, pre-positioning Iranian audiences to accept whatever MOU language Ghalibaf returns with as a "reasonable outcome given impossible US demands." The information environment is now the most contested it has been since the war began: Iran has a domestic channel again, diaspora outlets are filtered but not silenced, and the deal's domestic reception will be shaped by whichever narrative reaches Iranian citizens first.
